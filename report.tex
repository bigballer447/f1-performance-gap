\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
Formula~1 is a sport in which the machine matters at least as much as the
driver. For most of its history the grid has been sharply divided: a handful of
works teams fighting for victory and a tail of privateer entries several seconds
per lap slower. Since the 1990s the governing body has introduced increasingly
restrictive technical regulations, standardised components and, from 2021, a
budget cap, all with the stated aim of bringing the field closer together.

This report asks a quantitative version of that question: \textbf{has the lap-time
gap between the fastest and the slowest car on the grid become smaller over
time?} The analysis covers the seasons \firstYear--\lastYear, which is the period
for which individual lap times are available.

% =====================================================================
\section{Data sample}
% =====================================================================
We use the \emph{Formula~1 World Championship} dataset, a public release of the
Ergast motor-racing database hosted on Kaggle~\cite{dataset}. It contains every
championship race since 1950, but the four files used here restrict the usable
period:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item \texttt{lap\_times.csv} -- every individual lap, in milliseconds.
        Available only from 1996 onwards, which sets the start of our sample.
  \item \texttt{results.csv} -- links each driver in each race to the constructor
        whose car they drove.
  \item \texttt{races.csv} -- season, round and name of each event.
  \item \texttt{constructors.csv} -- constructor names.
\end{itemize}

The dataset is approximately \SI{40}{\mega\byte}, well within the limits of a
laptop. Three cleaning steps were applied. Lap times below \SI{30}{\second} or
above \SI{300}{\second} were discarded as they correspond to timing errors or to
cars limping back to the pits. Opening laps were removed, since they begin from a
standing start. Finally, a constructor was only counted in a race if at least
20~timed laps were recorded for it, so that a car which crashed early does not
contribute a meaningless ``best lap''. After cleaning, \nRaces{} races entered
the analysis.

% =====================================================================
\section{Analysis method}
% =====================================================================
For every race we take, for each constructor, the single fastest lap set by
either of its cars. Using the \emph{minimum} rather than the mean is deliberate:
a car's best lap is close to its true potential, whereas its average lap is
contaminated by traffic, fuel load and pit stops. The performance gap of a race
is then defined as the relative difference between the slowest and the fastest of
these constructor best laps,
%
\begin{equation}
  G = 100 \times \frac{t_{\text{slowest}} - t_{\text{fastest}}}{t_{\text{fastest}}}\ [\%],
\end{equation}
%
expressed as a percentage so that circuits of different lengths can be compared
directly. Races are aggregated per season using the \emph{median} of $G$, which
is insensitive to the small number of wet or safety-car-dominated events that
would distort a mean. The uncertainty on the median is estimated as
$1.253\,\sigma/\sqrt{N}$, with $\sigma$ the spread of the race values and $N$ the
number of races in the season.

The long-term trend is quantified by a weighted least-squares fit of a straight
line, $G(y) = a\,y + b$, to the season medians, where the slope $a$ is the
average change in the gap per year. The analysis is implemented in Python using
\texttt{pandas} for the data handling and \texttt{numpy} for the fit.

% =====================================================================
\section{Results and discussion}
% =====================================================================
Figure~\ref{fig:trend} shows the gap for every individual race, the season
medians, and the fitted trend. The gap falls from
$\gapFirst \pm \gapFirstErr\,\%$ in \firstYear{} to
$\gapLast \pm \gapLastErr\,\%$ in \lastYear.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{figures/gap_trend.png}
  \caption{Relative lap-time gap between the slowest and fastest constructor in
  each race (grey), season medians with their uncertainties (red), and the
  weighted linear fit (dashed).}
  \label{fig:trend}
\end{figure}

The fitted slope is
%
\begin{equation}
  a = \slope \pm \slopeErr \ \text{percentage points per year},
\end{equation}
%
with $\chi^2/\mathrm{ndf} = \chiNdf$. The slope differs from zero by
$\slopeSig\sigma$, so the shrinking of the field is statistically significant and
not a fluctuation. An independent cross-check comparing the first five seasons of
the sample with the last five gives a difference of
$\Delta G = \eraDiff \pm \eraDiffErr\,\%$ ($\eraSig\sigma$), consistent with the
fit.

Two caveats should be stated. First, the metric is sensitive to the composition
of the grid: the disappearance of small privateer teams after 2016 removes the
slowest cars from the sample and therefore contributes to the trend
independently of any convergence among the remaining teams. Second, the gap is
measured in race conditions, where a slow car may be lapped and run in clear air
while a fast car sits in traffic; qualifying times would be a cleaner comparison,
but would require a separate dataset.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Between \firstYear{} and \lastYear{} the lap-time gap
between the fastest and slowest Formula~1 car shrank at a rate of
$\slope \pm \slopeErr$ percentage points per year, a reduction of roughly a
factor of two over the period, and the effect is significant at the
$\slopeSig\sigma$ level.
